# lokul.club — Beyond the Pitch
### Everything That Gets Discussed Outside the Deck

> The deck gets you in the room. What happens after the deck determines whether you get the check.
> This covers the solo founder conversation, the "what else do you need" question, and everything investors probe that has nothing to do with your slides.

---

## Part 1: The Solo Founder Conversation

This will come up in every single meeting. Not always as a direct question — sometimes as a sideways comment, sometimes as a reference check topic. Be ready for all forms.

---

### The Direct Version
**"You're a solo founder. That's a significant concern for us. How do you think about that?"**

**What they're really asking:**
- If you burn out or get sick, is this over?
- Who challenges your thinking? Who tells you when you're wrong?
- Can you cover product + tech + ops + sales + fundraising simultaneously?
- What happens during the hard months — and there will be hard months?

**The weak answer (avoid):**
> "I work better alone / I prefer to move fast / I haven't found the right co-founder yet."

All three signal either defensiveness, poor judgment, or that you've been trying and failing to recruit a co-founder — which raises a different question.

**The strong answer:**
> "I'm a solo founder today by choice, not by accident. Early on I learned that the wrong co-founder is far more damaging than no co-founder — I've seen companies destroy themselves over co-founder disputes. My approach is to build a founding team without equity co-founders: a strong first hire who covers the technical or community ops gap, a 3-person advisory board with specific domain roles, and one investor-partner relationship where I get the intellectual challenge a co-founder would provide. I am actively looking for someone to grow into a co-founder role — someone who earns it through execution rather than someone I recruit on day one because the cap table needs to look a certain way."

**Then turn it:**
> "I'll be honest — one of the things I'm specifically looking for from this conversation is whether you can introduce me to someone with ground-level community ops experience in Indian markets who might be that co-founding operator. That's more valuable to me right now than almost anything else."

---

### The Indirect Version
**"Tell me about your team."**
*(When the answer is "it's me right now")*

**How to handle it:**
Don't apologize. Describe your current support structure and your 90-day hiring plan in the same breath.

> "Right now it's me, plus two part-time contributors — [specific roles]. My first two hires with this funding are: (1) a community ops lead with on-ground experience in Indian apartment societies, and (2) a full-stack engineer who has built marketplace or community products before. I've shortlisted 3 candidates for each role. I can close both within 60 days of the check clearing."

**Key principle:** Investors know solo founders can work. Airbnb had 3 founders but one did almost everything. WhatsApp had 2. What they need to know is that you understand your gaps and have a specific plan to fill them — not a vague "I'll hire people."

---

### The Reference Check Version
After the meeting, they will call people who know you. Not just the references you give — they will find people through mutual connections.

**What they will ask those people:**
- "Is this person coachable? Do they take feedback?"
- "Do they follow through on what they say they'll do?"
- "What's their relationship with stress and setbacks?"
- "Would you work for them?"
- "Is there a reason they don't have a co-founder?"

**How to prepare:**
Think about the 5 people in your professional life who know you best and would speak most honestly. Reach out to them before the pitch, not after. Tell them you're fundraising. Ask if they'd be comfortable being a reference. This prevents surprises.

## Part 2: "Apart from Funding — What Else Are You Looking For?"

This is the most underrated question in any investor meeting. Most founders say "mentorship" or "network" and move on. That is a wasted opportunity.

This question is the investor telling you: **I have things besides money. Show me you know what you need.**

The right answer demonstrates:
1. Self-awareness about your specific gaps
2. Strategic thinking about what kind of partner will actually help
3. That you're evaluating them as much as they're evaluating you

---

### The Full Answer (use parts of this, not all of it at once)

**"Three things specifically:"**

**1. A co-founding operator introduction**
> "I'm looking for someone with 3–5 years of ground-level community or marketplace ops in India — ideally someone who's built city-level supply in a hyperlocal context. Not a consultant, not an advisor — someone who wants to build. If you have that person in your portfolio or network, that introduction is worth more to me than the check."

*Why this works:* It shows you know your exact gap and aren't just fishing for a generic intro.

---

**2. Warm access to RWA and housing networks**
> "My first 500 lokul activations will be in organized apartment societies. If you have LPs, portfolio founders, or co-investors who are connected to large RWA federations, cooperative housing societies, or property management companies in Mumbai, Bengaluru, or Bhopal — those introductions compress my activation timeline by months."

*Why this works:* It's specific, actionable, and shows you've thought about the go-to-market bottleneck, not just the product.

---

**3. An investor who will tell me when I'm wrong**
> "I'm a solo founder. The honest risk is that I don't have someone challenging my thinking daily. I'm not looking for a board seat or formal governance — I'm looking for an investor who will call me after they see a competitor move or a market signal and say 'have you thought about this?' I want a thinking partner, not a check and a quarterly update."

*Why this works:* It's the most human answer. It shows vulnerability without weakness. Almost every investor will remember this line.

---

### What NOT to Say
| Weak answer | Why it's weak |
|---|---|
| "Mentorship and guidance" | Too vague. Every founder says this. |
| "Your network" | Also vague. Network for what, specifically? |
| "Help with hiring" | Slightly better. But still generic. |
| "Distribution partnerships" | Good direction but needs specifics. |
| "Nothing — the money is enough" | Signals you don't understand what good investors bring. |

## Part 3: The Conversations That Happen Outside the Deck

These are the topics that come up in the informal parts of the meeting — coffee before, lunch after, the last 10 minutes when the deck is closed.

---

### "Why this? Why now? Why you?"

This is the personal story question. They want to know if you have a lived experience with the problem — or if you found it in a research report.

**Prepare a real answer.** Specifically:
- Have you personally struggled to find a reliable plumber in your building?
- Have you seen a neighbor run an informal home food business with no digital presence?
- Have you watched a kirana lose customers to Blinkit and felt that was wrong?

**The answer that works:**
> A specific, personal moment. Not a macro trend you read about. One scene, one person, one problem you witnessed that you couldn't stop thinking about.

Investors fund people before products. The personal story is the foundation.

---

### "What does success look like for you personally in 5 years?"

They're checking vision alignment and ambition calibration.

**Too small:** "I want to build a sustainable lifestyle business in my city."
*(They're writing a pre-seed check for a 100x return, not a lifestyle business)*

**Too large:** "We'll be bigger than Zomato and JioMart combined."
*(Sounds unmoored from reality)*

**The right zone:**
> "In 5 years, lokul is the default neighborhood OS for 10 million Indian households — the thing you open when you need something from your neighborhood, the way you open Google Maps when you need directions. We're either the category leader that gets acquired by a super-app for the neighborhood graph, or we're large enough to IPO as an infrastructure company for local commerce. Either outcome is a strong return for a pre-seed bet."

---

### "What keeps you up at night?"

This is the self-awareness test. There is no right answer — only honest and dishonest ones.

**The honest answer:**
Pick your single most real concern. Not a humble-brag fear ("we might grow too fast"). An actual operational or existential worry.

Examples of real answers:
> "The caste and religious dynamics in Indian neighborhoods. I know our platform will surface community tensions that exist offline, and I don't have a complete playbook for that yet."

> "The activation model for non-gated neighborhoods. Our MVP works in organized societies. 90% of India doesn't live in organized societies. That's the gap that I think about most."

> "Being a solo founder in a ground-level ops business. There will be a month when three things go wrong simultaneously and I won't have a co-founder to divide the load with."

**Why this works:** It shows you know what the real risks are. Investors don't expect you to have solved everything — they expect you to know what you haven't solved.

---

### "Who else is in the round? Who else are you talking to?"

This is a social proof and competitive urgency question.

**If you have other investors committed or interested:**
> Name them if you have permission, or say: "I have a couple of angels who have indicated interest — I'm not ready to name them until term sheets are signed, but the round is moving."

**If you're early in conversations:**
> "I'm in active conversations with 4–5 investors. You're one of the first institutional conversations — I wanted to start with people whose portfolio suggests they understand Indian community platforms."

**What to never say:** "You're the first person I've talked to." It removes urgency and signals you have no other options.

---

### Valuation Discussion

This will happen, usually at the end of a promising first meeting or in a follow-up.

**Pre-seed India benchmarks (2025–2026):**
- Typical pre-seed valuation range: ₹8–20 Cr (post-money) for $500K–$1M
- At $750K raise: a ₹12–15 Cr post-money valuation gives ~5–6% dilution — reasonable
- SAFE notes are common at pre-seed; valuation caps of ₹12–20 Cr are typical

**What to say when asked for your number:**
> "We're thinking about a SAFE with a ₹15 Cr post-money cap and a 20% discount. That gives us enough runway for 18 months to reach Series A-ready metrics without over-diluting at this stage."

**If they push back on the cap:**
> "I'm open to discussing structure. What I care more about is closing the round with the right partners in the next 6 weeks — getting to proof points matters more than optimizing the cap at this stage."

---

### "What happens if this doesn't work?"

Investors ask this to understand resilience and whether you have a fallback that makes you less desperate — or a personality that can handle failure.

**The wrong answer:** "That won't happen" or "I haven't thought about it."

**The right answer:**
> "If in 18 months the core community-to-commerce loop isn't working, I'll know early enough to pivot the product toward the one vertical that is working — likely either society management software (competing with MyGate) or a peer services marketplace in a single category. Both are smaller but viable businesses. I'm not betting on a binary outcome."

## Part 4: The Post-Meeting Process — What Happens After You Leave

Most founders forget that the deal is still being made after they walk out. Here's what actually happens:

---

### The Partner Meeting
After a positive first meeting, you'll be asked to present to the full partner group. This is different from a one-on-one:

- More people means more attack vectors — prepare for someone who hasn't read anything about you
- One partner will play devil's advocate — expect the hardest questions here
- They will have discussed your weaknesses among themselves before you enter the room

**Prepare differently for this:** Ask your first contact explicitly: "Who will be in the room and what do you think their main concern will be?" Most investors will tell you.

---

### The Reference Check (Happens in Parallel)
While you think the deal is in discussion, they are already calling people. The reference check is not a formality — it has killed deals that seemed certain.

**Who they call:**
- People you named as references
- People they find through mutual LinkedIn connections
- Former employers or colleagues from your visible history
- If you mentioned a pilot customer, they may reach out to them

**What you can do:** Pre-brief your references. Tell them: "An investor named [X] may call you. They'll want to know about my execution and whether I take feedback well. Here are the two or three things I'd want you to highlight."

---

### The "Second Thoughts" Email
Investors sometimes send a follow-up email with 3–5 questions after the meeting. This is a good sign — they're doing diligence, not ghosting you.

**How to respond:**
- Within 24 hours
- Numbered answers, not paragraph prose
- One data point per answer where possible
- End with: "Happy to jump on a call for any of these if it would be faster."

---

### The Silence
Sometimes you hear nothing for 2–3 weeks. This does not mean a pass — it often means they're discussing internally or waiting to see another meeting.

**What to do:**
Send one follow-up at 10 days with a genuine new piece of information — a pilot metric update, a new lokul activated, a press mention. Not "just checking in." Give them a reason to re-engage.

---

## Part 5: The Human Questions — Often Asked, Rarely Prepared For

---

**"What does your family think about you doing this?"**
They're checking for support structure and whether personal obligations create risk.

**"Have you done this full-time? What did you leave?"**
They want to know your skin in the game and what you sacrificed.

**"Who do you call when something goes really wrong at 11pm?"**
They're asking about your support network and resilience infrastructure.

**"What's the last thing you were badly wrong about?"**
Pure self-awareness and intellectual honesty test.

**"If I gave you ₹10 Cr today instead of ₹5 Cr — what would you do differently?"**
Tests capital discipline and whether you'd blow the money or focus.

---

## The Summary: What Investors Are Really Evaluating

At pre-seed, the product is almost secondary. What they're actually evaluating is:

```
1. Do I believe this person will figure it out?         (40%)
2. Is the market real and large enough?                 (25%)
3. Is the product directionally right?                  (20%)
4. Can I help them and will they listen?                (15%)
```

The solo founder risk lives in #1 and #4. Every conversation you have should be reinforcing that you have the judgment, resilience, and self-awareness to figure it out — and that you're actively building the team and advisors around you to compensate for what you don't know.

---

## The Three Things to Say in Every Meeting That Solo Founders Don't Usually Say

1. **"Here's the one thing I'm most wrong about and what I'm doing to find out."**
   *(Shows intellectual honesty — rare in founders)*

2. **"I'm specifically looking for an investor who will tell me when I'm headed in the wrong direction. Not just a check."**
   *(Shows you understand the value of a thinking partner — turns the solo founder risk into an investor opportunity)*

3. **"The one thing I can't buy with this money is what I'm hoping you can help me find: [specific person / specific intro / specific knowledge]."**
   *(Shows you've thought about what they bring beyond capital — and gives them a specific way to add value immediately)*
